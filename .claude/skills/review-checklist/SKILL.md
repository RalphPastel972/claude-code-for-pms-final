# Review checklist for a brief

The user is a product manager who wants a quick, honest check before a brief goes to someone busy (a director, engineers, designers). Those readers often stop after the first page, so the four checks below are the ones that most often decide whether a brief gets acted on. Your job is to check them, show the evidence, and suggest small fixes. It is not to rewrite the brief.

## Steps

1. **Find the brief.** Use the file or text the user points at. If they don't say which, look for the brief they've been working on in this session (for example `brief.md`); if there's more than one candidate, ask which.
2. **Read the whole brief**, including tables, footnotes and any linked file in the same folder (a linked prototype counts as part of what the brief promises).
3. **Run the four checks** below. For each one, decide **Pass**, **Partial** or **Fail**, and quote the exact line(s) that justify it. If nothing in the brief addresses a check, say so and quote nothing.
4. **Report** in the format below. Don't edit the brief unless the user asks; offer to apply the fixes instead.

Reply in the user's language. Keep the report short enough to read in two minutes.

## The four checks

### 1. Names who owns it
A reader should know, without asking, which named person is responsible for this brief and its next step.
- **Pass:** a named person is clearly the owner (e.g. "Owner: Ralph, PM Dispatch").
- **Partial:** only a role or team ("the PM", "Dispatch team"), or names that are the *audience* or reviewers rather than the owner ("Draft for Helen" says who it's for, not who owns it), or owners are named for some open items but not for the brief itself.
- **Fail:** no owner anywhere.

### 2. Says how we'll know it worked
Success has to be checkable later by someone who wasn't in the room.
- **Pass:** at least one measurable signal, with where it is today (baseline), what "worked" looks like (target), and who checks it or when.
- **Partial:** a measure without a baseline or target, a target without a time or person, or only vague goals ("improve", "better experience").
- **Fail:** no success measure.
Note any measure that tracks something the brief doesn't propose to change; that also matters for check 3.

### 3. Keeps the same scope start to finish
The problem, what's proposed, how success is measured and what's left open should all be about the same thing.
Look for:
- a problem described early that the proposal never addresses;
- a proposed item with no matching problem;
- a success measure for something outside the proposal;
- something declared out of scope that comes back later;
- the title or summary promising more (or less) than the body delivers.
- **Pass:** none of these. **Partial:** minor drift (one item). **Fail:** the brief is really about two different things, or the ending contradicts the beginning.

### 4. Explains the problem before the fix
Readers decide whether a fix makes sense only after they understand the problem.
- **Pass:** before any proposed change, the brief says who is affected, what goes wrong for them, and some evidence (a number, a quote, an example).
- **Partial:** the problem comes first but has no evidence, or a solution is named in the opening lines (a title that states the goal is fine; a title that names the mechanism is not).
- **Fail:** the brief opens with the solution, or never states the problem.

## Report format

Use this structure:

**Verdict:** one sentence — ready to send, ready after small fixes, or needs rework.

| Check | Result | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Names who owns it | Pass / Partial / Fail | short quote or "not found" |
| Says how we'll know it worked | … | … |
| Keeps the same scope start to finish | … | … |
| Explains the problem before the fix | … | … |

**Fixes**, one per check that isn't a Pass, most important first: what to change and a suggested wording, in one or two lines each.

**Not checked:** one line reminding the user this covers four checks only, not whether the facts and numbers are right.
